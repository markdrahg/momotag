The Problem

A four-step leak, hiding in plain sight.

Most mobile money systems display a recipient's full registered name during transfer confirmation — even when the transfer is never completed. Almost no one treats this as an attack surface.

Probe

A stranger, knowing only your phone number, opens a mobile money app and starts a transfer to you. No money needs to be sent.

Disclose

Before confirming, the app displays your full registered name for the sender to verify — standard "confirm recipient" behaviour in most MoMo systems.

Capture

The stranger notes or screenshots your name, then cancels the transfer. Nothing was sent, nothing was logged as suspicious, and you were never notified.

Exploit

Your real name — now confirmed and tied to your phone number — can be used to impersonate you, fabricate claims in your name, or run targeted scams on people who know you.

Why this matters
  • The harvesting step costs the attacker nothing and leaves no record of malicious intent — a cancelled transaction looks identical to a genuine mistake.
  • It can be automated: sequential phone numbers can be probed to build a database of real names at scale.
  • Awareness is extremely low — roughly 1 in 100 users has likely ever considered this as an attack surface.
The Solution

Introducing MoMoTag.

A persistent, non-identifying alias stands in for a user's full legal name at the transaction-confirmation layer. The legal name never leaves the system — it just stops being shown to an unrelated sender.

Today
"Confirm transfer of GHS 50 to Mark Drah?"
With MoMoTag
"Confirm transfer of GHS 50 to MD-4471?"
Tier 1 · Default

Automatic, for everyone

Every user is assigned a system-generated alias at registration — no action required. This guarantees baseline privacy by default, not just for the privacy-conscious.

Tier 2 · Optional

Customizable, for recognisability

Users may personalise the readable portion of their alias (e.g. "MARKD"). The numeric suffix stays system-enforced to guarantee uniqueness — with a change-frequency limit to prevent abuse.

How It Works

Four steps, start to finish.

The alias is bound to a person's verified identity — not their SIM — so it persists across phone changes and never breaks a contact who already trusts it.

1

Registration

KYC completed as usual; the system auto-generates a unique alias alongside it.

2

Confirmation

Senders see the alias only — the legal name is never shown to an unrelated party.

3

Identity binding

The alias is tied to verified identity, not SIM — it survives phone and number changes.

4

Trusted contact

After a successful transaction, the alias is saved — repeat transfers are verified by history.

MoMoTag deliberately does not attempt to solve first-contact identity verification — a broader problem that today's full-name display doesn't fully solve either.

Instead, it targets one specific problem well: passive, automated, no-consent name harvesting.

What Doesn't Change

A display-layer fix, not a data-layer one.

MoMoTag changes what an unrelated sender sees on a confirmation screen. It does not touch what's stored, what regulators can access, or what law enforcement can investigate.

Display layer — this is what MoMoTag changes
What an unrelated sender sees during a transfer-confirmation screen: real name → persistent alias
Data layer — untouched
Full legal name · KYC records · Compliance access · Law-enforcement access

Full legal names remain stored and tied to verified KYC records, exactly as regulation requires today.

Account holders can always view their own real name and alias pairing.

Telecom compliance and fraud-detection systems retain full access to real identity data.

Law enforcement retains access to real identity data through proper legal process.

Scope & Honesty

What this proposal claims — and doesn't.

A proposal is more credible when it's honest about its edges. Here's exactly what MoMoTag is solving, and what it deliberately leaves open.

What MoMoTag solves

Passive, automated, no-consent harvesting of real names via fake or cancelled transfer attempts — at zero cost and zero trace to the attacker.

What it doesn't claim to solve

First-contact identity verification — confirming with certainty that an alias belongs to the person a sender intends to pay, before any transaction history exists. This limitation already exists in today's full-name systems too.

Advantages

Why this is worth building.

01

Privacy by default

Every user gains protection automatically — no opt-in, no need to understand the risk first.

02

Harder to scrape

Removes the ability to cheaply script large-scale collection of real names tied to phone numbers.

03

Regulator-friendly

No change to KYC, AML, or law-enforcement access — a far easier path to real-world adoption.

04

Portable identity

Alias binding to KYC identity, not SIM, means protection survives SIM swaps and number changes.

05

Built-in branding

Tier 2 customization lets merchants use a recognisable, trustworthy alias instead of a random code.

06

Low friction

Senders still confirm recipients before paying — the flow barely changes, only what's displayed does.
